# The case for building in public

## Why I started sharing everything

Three years ago I shipped a product nobody wanted. I spent eight months polishing it in private, convinced that secrecy was protecting some enormous advantage, and when I finally launched, the silence was total. Not criticism. Silence. Eleven signups, four of them friends being kind.

The autopsy was uncomfortable. Every wrong assumption I'd baked into the product could have been caught in week two by simply showing someone what I was making. The interface everyone found confusing. The pricing model that made sense only to me. The core feature that solved a problem almost nobody actually had. Eight months of compounding on mistakes that one honest conversation would have surfaced.

So the next project went the other way. I posted the first sketch before writing a line of code. I shared revenue numbers when they were embarrassing, which was most months. I wrote up every architectural decision, including the ones that blew up in my face. And the strangest thing happened: people started caring before there was anything to care about.

## What actually changes

The obvious benefit is feedback, but that's not the real story. The real story is that building in public changes what you build.

When you know you'll write about a decision, you make the decision differently. Shortcuts you'd quietly take alone become embarrassing to describe, so you stop taking them. Vague plans you'd let drift become concrete the moment you have to explain them to strangers. It's the same effect as explaining a bug to a colleague and solving it mid-sentence — except it applies to everything, all the time.

The second change is speed, in a direction you don't expect. You ship smaller things sooner, because a public build with nothing to show is a broken promise. My private project had a six-week gap where I refactored the data layer and produced nothing visible. The public one never went more than a week without something someone could react to. Same hours, wildly different momentum.

The third change is the audience itself. The people who follow a build are not customers yet. They're closer to collaborators. They've watched the reasoning, argued with parts of it, seen their suggestions land in the changelog. By launch day they aren't discovering the product, they're celebrating something they helped steer. My first launch had eleven signups. The public one had four hundred on day one, and I knew half of them by name.

## The costs nobody mentions

It isn't free. Writing takes real time — call it four hours a week, honestly accounted. Some weeks the update is the only thing that ships, and you feel like a commentator on your own absence.

Competitors see everything. In practice this mattered less than I feared. Ideas are cheap and execution is visible to everyone anyway once you ship. But there was one genuinely painful moment when a better-funded team lifted a feature design wholesale, shipped it first, and got the press for it. That stung for a month.

The harder cost is emotional. Public numbers mean public bad months. When revenue dropped forty percent last spring, I had to write that paragraph, and the writing of it was worse than the drop. There's a version of building in public that curates only the wins, and everyone can smell it. The whole value is in the uncomfortable parts, which means signing up for discomfort on a schedule.

And some things genuinely can't be shared. Customer conversations, security details, anything involving another person's livelihood. The line takes judgment, and you only find it by occasionally stepping over it and apologising.

## How to start without drowning

If any of this sounds appealing, start smaller than feels meaningful. One update a week, fixed day, no exceptions. The fixed day matters more than the content — it converts sharing from a decision into a habit, and decisions are what burn you out.

Write the update you'd want to read: what you tried, what happened, what you're doing about it. Skip the inspirational framing. Nobody following a build wants a keynote; they want the diff.

Share the decision before it's final when you can. A question gets engagement that an announcement never will, and the answers are free consulting from people who owe you nothing. The times I asked "should the free tier include this" taught me more about my market than any survey.

Finally, keep a private layer. Not everything benefits from an audience, and knowing you have a place to think in the dark makes the public parts more honest, not less.

## Where this lands

I still don't think building in public is a growth hack, and the people selling it as one are mostly selling courses. It's slower than it looks, more uncomfortable than the highlight reels suggest, and useless without something real underneath.

What it actually is, is a forcing function for honesty — with your users about what you're making, and with yourself about how it's going. The eight months I spent building the wrong thing in private weren't a marketing failure. They were an honesty failure, and an audience is simply the cheapest honesty machine I've found.

Ship the update. Especially the one you don't want to write. That's the tour, that's the pitch, and that's everything I know about it so far.
